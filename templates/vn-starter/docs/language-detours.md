# Optional language detours

Learner feedback asks for short linguistic side quests inside conversations: an unfamiliar expression, a plausible near miss, or a fitting idiom can reveal something beyond the basic translation. Use this as an authoring pattern for new or revised choices within the requested scope. It is not a new runtime mechanic or an instruction to rewrite the airport scene.

## Design principles

- **Offer curiosity alongside a familiar path.** Where the scene benefits, offer a familiar response and an optional expression that explores meaning, politeness, register, or who says what to whom. A plausible near miss can be another option. This is not a mandatory three-option formula, and not every choice needs a detour. Preserve an established English fallback where appropriate.
- **Teach one useful distinction.** Selecting the expression leads to a short, character-appropriate reaction and a concise explanation or clarification, then rejoins the conversation. Let the learner discover something even when the attempt is awkward. Avoid a lecture, repeated testing, humiliation, or a generic incorrect screen. Brief detours should fit the chapter's pacing and usual one-to-three significant language interactions; do not use optional branches to hide an excessive lesson load.
- **Judge conversational fit, not sophistication.** Distinguish grammatical error, understandable but mismatched usage, appropriate alternatives, and differences in tone. More complex Mandarin is not automatically more correct. If several expressions work, acknowledge them; a character's preference is not a universal language rule. The learner should understand why a near miss is a near miss and when it would work.
- **Support exploration.** Use characters, tone-marked pinyin, and natural English meaning by default. The English meaning can be visible when the learning goal is choosing an appropriate conversational response. Withhold support only under the deliberate reveal rules in [Mandarin presentation](mandarin-presentation.md). Do not expect mastery of a newly introduced idiom or an optional earlier branch. All routes retain forward progress; any later callback must account for whether this lesson was seen.
- **Use idioms selectively.** Chengyu (成语, chéngyǔ; established Chinese idioms) may appear as a suitable stretch option or a plausible mismatch when their actual meaning, register, and usage fit the lesson. Explain the intended meaning and, only when helpful, the literal image or origin. Verify idiomatic usage with reliable language sources; do not invent an origin or equate any four-character expression with a chengyu. Everyday expressions are equally valuable.
- **Separate learning from romance.** Specify language rewards, including zero, rather than granting extra XP merely for picking the longest or unfamiliar phrase. Curiosity can pay off through new understanding and a memorable exchange. For personal choices involving romantic interests, every option still needs meaningful relationship effects under the existing rule; tie those effects to emotional intent, attention, honesty, or respected boundaries, separately from correctness. A recoverable language slip need not punish the relationship.

## Illustrative luggage exchange

Assume Emma is responding to Li Wei helping with her luggage. These are candidate teaching contrasts, not approved replacement dialogue or a complete option set:

| Expression   | Pinyin          | Meaning                                     | Lesson and possible treatment                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------ | --------------- | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 谢谢。       | Xièxie.         | Thank you.                                  | A direct, appropriate expression of gratitude; it is not inferior because it is simple.                                                                                                                                                                         |
| 你太客气了！ | Nǐ tài kèqi le! | You're too kind / too polite!               | A context-dependent comment on the other person's courtesy. It can also respond to thanks or a compliment; do not label it a universally better version of “thank you.” Writer must make its intended reading clear in the surrounding exchange.                |
| 别客气。     | Bié kèqi.       | Don't mention it / no need to be so polite. | Commonly the helper's response to thanks. As Emma's attempted thanks here, it switches conversational roles: valid Mandarin, mismatched use. Li Wei could briefly tease that she took his reply, then clarify who normally says it; the conversation continues. |

The proposed character reactions are design suggestions. The usage distinction is supported by [Michigan State University's _Elementary Chinese II_, Lesson 9](https://openbooks.lib.msu.edu/chs102/open/download?type=print_pdf#page=33), which models the helper's reply and discusses gratitude responses on PDF page 46. The context-dependent readings of the courtesy expression are also recorded in [Trainchinese's dictionary entry](https://www.trainchinese.com/v2/wordDetails.php?rAp=0&tcLanguage=en&wordId=70839). These examples are everyday courtesy expressions, not chengyu examples.

## Production and acceptance

Writer identifies a fitting opportunity and authors each response, reaction, brief explanation, support text, and branch merge. Director records the learning distinction, contextual validity of every option, whether it is exploration or a graded exercise, display/reveal rules, audio coverage, rewards, and reuse of shared choice/feedback behavior in the existing plan and specification. No new UI or persistence system is implied by the term “side quest.”

Apply the [canonical speech rules](mandarin-presentation.md#speech-rules): every standalone Chinese word, phrase, or sentence spoken by a character requires a matching recording in that character's assigned voice, even when pinyin or an English translation appears on the same screen and even without a comprehension game. Determine coverage from the spoken utterance, not the support layout; intentionally silent reading and written props remain distinct. Director plans it; Developer uses approved reuse or the provider workflow and exact cast/model/settings currently assigned in [the voice cast](../story/voice-cast.md#generation-model-assignments) for new clips. Audio does not replace text support.

QA reads every option with its lead-in, reaction, explanation, and merge. Verify linguistic and social-context accuracy, useful feedback on near misses, acceptance of valid alternatives, manageable difficulty, and a familiar route that does not force the detour. Check support/audio and any actual reward, save/resume, and replay behavior. An intentionally awkward response must never be taught as ungrammatical merely because it belongs in a different conversational role. Document implemented detours and newly taught expressions during mandatory chapter closeout; keep proposals separate from shipped behavior.
